II. 
Legal Standard 
The United Nations Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign 
Arbitral Awards, also known as the New York Convention, “applies to arbitral awards 
‘made’ in a foreign country that a party seeks to enforce in the United States.”  CBF 
Industria de Gusa S/A v. AMCI Holdings, Inc., 850 F.3d 58, 70 (2d Cir. 2017).  The 
Federal Arbitration Act incorporates the provisions of the New York Convention into the 
United States Code.  See 9 U.S.C. §§ 201–208.  Like the United States, the People’s 
Republic of China has ratified the New York Convention.  Liu Luwei v. Phyto Tech 
Corp., No. CV 18-2174-JFW(GJSx), 2018 WL 6016958, *2 (C.D. Cal. June 18, 2018) 
(citation omitted).

A district court’s review of a foreign arbitral award “is strictly limited.”  Pacer 
Construction Holdings Corp. v. Pelletier, No. 19-cv-1263-MMA (BGS), 2020 WL 
977871, at *2 (S.D. Cal. Feb. 28, 2020) (citation omitted); Luwei, 2018 WL 6016958, at 
*2 (“When considering petitions to recognize a foreign arbitration award, courts have 
‘little discretion.’” (citation omitted)).   
 
Article V of the New York Convention “sets out five grounds for refusal that may 
be raised by parties and two grounds that may be granted by the court sua sponte.”  
Changzhou AMEC Eastern Tools and Equipment Co., Ltd v. Eastern Tools & Equipment, 
Inc., No. EDCV 11-00354 VAP (DTBx), 2012 WL 3106620, at *8 (C.D. Cal. July 30, 
2012) (citation omitted).  “Respondent has the burden of establishing a defense against 
enforcement under the Convention because there is a strong presumption in favor of 
confirmation of arbitral awards.”  Id. (citing Polimaster Ltd. v. RAE Sys., Inc., 623 F.3d 
832, 836 (9th Cir. 2010)).  “The presumption in favor of confirmation is based on the 
goal of the Convention, which is to encourage the recognition of international arbitral 
agreements.”  Id. (citing Scherk v. Alberto-Culver Co., 417 U.S. 506, 520, n.15 (1974)).  
Therefore, “courts have adopted a narrow view of the defenses enumerated in the 
Convention.”  Id. (citation omitted).  “Absent a convincing showing that one of [the 
Convention’s] narrow exceptions applies, the arbitral award will be confirmed.”  Id. 
(citations omitted).

III. 
Discussion 
Respondent opposes confirmation of Petitioner’s arbitration award on three 
grounds: (1) Respondent was not provided proper notice of the proceedings or an 
opportunity to be heard; (2) enforcement of the award contains an unenforceable 
liquidated damages clause; and (3) the arbitration award entered against Respondent 
includes attorney fees for Petitioner in violation of the “American Rule.”  See generally 
Opp.  The Court addresses each argument in turn. 
 
A. 
Proper Notice 
Respondent argues he never received proper notice of the arbitration of the 
proceedings under Article V of the New York Convention.  Id. 5:7–8:3.  Respondent 
argues that CIETAC sent arbitration notice to two addresses, both of which returned the 
notice as undeliverable.  Id.  Respondent asserts that the arbitration award merely 
concludes “notarized service” was provided, without indicating how the documents were 
served or who received the documents.  Id.  Respondent claims he was not aware of the 
arbitration proceedings, which took place in China, because he had been living in 
California since January 2021.  Dkt. # 20-2, ¶¶ 5–7.   
 
Petitioner responds that proper notice—rather than actual notice—is sufficient and 
that CIETAC effectuated proper notice pursuant to the CIETAC Rules, including sending 
service to both Respondent’s last known residential address (now his parents’ address) 
and his last known business address (the address of Erdong, the business at the center of 
the Transfer Agreement).  Reply 9:17–12:18.  Petitioner further asserts that Respondent 
knew Erdong had not launched a successful IPO in the Transfer Agreement’s 12-month 
timeline, and therefore Respondent had actual notice that Petitioner would ask to 
repurchase shares, yet Respondent failed to respond to Petitioner’s messages and left the 
country without properly updating his change of address.  Id. 12:19–13:25.

Courts have interpreted the New York Convention’s proper notice standard as 
requiring compliance with federal due process.  See Linley Invs. v. Jamgotchian, No. CV 
11-724 JAK (RZx), 2012 WL 12953824, at *4 (C.D. Cal. May 11, 2012). “The 
fundamental requirement of due process is the opportunity to be heard at a ‘meaningful 
time and in a meaningful manner.’”  Iran Aircraft Indus. V. Avco Corp., 980 F.2d 141, 
146 (2d Cir. 1992) (quoting Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 333 (1976)).  Key to this 
requirement is whether notice was “reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to 
apprise interested persons of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to 
present their objections.”  Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust. Co., 339 U.S. 306, 
314 (1950) (citations omitted); see also Linley Invs. v. Jamgotchian, 670 F. App’x 627, 
628 (9th Cir. 2016) (applying this standard to confirm foreign arbitration award).  
  
In international arbitration cases, even when respondents allege lack of actual 
notice, courts have determined that proper notice was provided if service was attempted 
and was reasonably calculated to provide notice to the respondents.  See Linley, 2012 WL 
12953824, at *4 (finding notice sent to respondent’s listed email address and mailing 
address satisfied due process requirements, even though respondent contended he did not 
receive actual notice because he was traveling and his fax machine was unplugged); 
Tianjin Port Free Trade Zone Int’l Trade Service Co., Ltd., v. Tiancheng Int’l Inc., No. 
ED CV 17-2127 PA (SHKx), 2018 WL 4502497, at *4 (C.D. Cal. Sept. 18, 2018) 
(determining that although respondent claimed he did not receive notice, CIETAC’s 
arbitration award had “detail[ed] CIETAC’s efforts to provide notice,” including sending 
four notices by express mail to respondent’s address); Weihai Textile Group Import & 
Export Co., Ltd. v. Eruption Holdings, Inc., No. 21-MC-147-SWS, 2022 WL 18141749, 
at *7–8 (D. Wyo. Mar. 4, 2022) (finding multiple mailings to respondent from the 
arbitrator were reasonably calculated to provide proper notice to respondent: “Absent 
convincing evidence to the contrary . . . the Court defers to the arbitrator’s statements in 
the arbitral award of having mailed multiple notices . . . and confirmed their delivery”); 
Shanghai Liyu Optoelectronic Co. v. Brite Lite Tribe, LLC, No. 24-CV-80690-RLR, 2025 
WL 1040836, at *6. (S.D. Fla. Apr. 8, 2025) (holding that attempted service was proper 
where petitioner had complied with the Shanghai arbitration’s notice procedures, 
petitioner did not know respondent had changed its primary physical address, and 
petitioner had attempted to send emails to respondent).

B. 
Liquidated Damages Clause 
Respondent contends that the Court should deny enforcement of CIETAC’s 
arbitration award because CIETAC applied a contract with an improper liquidated 
damages clause, which is an unreasonable penalty under California law and public policy.  
Id. 8:10–9:10.   
 
As an initial matter, Respondent’s argument that enforcing a liquidated damages 
clause is against the public policy of California “has no relevance” because it is the 
public policy of the United States that is at issue.  See Dandong Shuguang Axel Corp., 
Ltd. v. Brilliance Machinery Co., No. C 00-4480 SC, 2001 WL 637446, at *6 (N.D. Cal. 
June 1, 2001) (granting petition to enforce international arbitration, including $100,000 in 
penalties, and noting: “[W]hether assessing punitive damages in arbitration is against the 
public policy or prohibited in New York law has no relevance to the question of whether 
enforcing the [international arbitration commission’s] punitive damages is contrary to 
public policy of the United States”).  Moreover, courts uphold punitive damages awards 
in arbitration, even when a state law or policy against them would otherwise preclude 
enforcement.  See id. (noting the Supreme Court has held that the Federal Arbitration Act 
“preempts state law so that when the arbitration rules allow punitive damages, a state law 
or policy against them will not preclude enforcement” and “federal courts, including the 
Ninth Circuit, have upheld punitive damages awarded in arbitration”).  Respondent has 
provided no pertinent authority to the contrary. 
 
Accordingly, the Court finds that enforcement of this arbitration award, including 
its application of a liquidated damages clause, is not contrary to the public policy of the 
United States.

parties’ contract and does not run contrary to the public policy of the United States. 
 
IV. 
Conclusion 
For the foregoing reasons, the Court GRANTS Petitioner’s second amended 
petition to confirm the arbitration award and for entry of judgment and GRANTS leave 
for Petitioner’s untimely declaration.  Petitioner shall submit a proposed judgment 
consistent with this Order no later than August 1, 2025. 
IT IS SO ORDERED.
